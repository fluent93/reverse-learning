COMMENTS FOR THE AUTHOR:

Reviewer #1: Overall, this manuscript presents a timely, relevant, and potentially impactful conceptual contribution to the growing literature on generative AI in education. The proposed Reverse Learning Framework shifts the conversation from a narrow focus on AI detection and academic misconduct toward a more productive emphasis on learner ownership, epistemic responsibility, and accountable AI use. At the same time, several substantive issues require further development.
The primary area for improvement concerns the framework's theoretical contribution and novelty. Although the manuscript distinguishes Reverse Learning from AI literacy, productive failure, and related concepts, it does not fully establish what is uniquely explained by the framework beyond integrating existing ideas into a sequential process. Theoretical foundations such as AI literacy, metacognition, ICAP, and productive failure are discussed, but stronger integration is needed to explain how these theories collectively support the framework and the mechanisms through which learners move from AI-generated outputs to ownership and understanding. The author(s) could more explicitly articulate (1) What new explanatory mechanism Reverse Learning introduces, (2) Why existing frameworks cannot adequately explain this process, and (3) What educational phenomena are uniquely captured by Reverse Learning.
A second major concern involves learner readiness and prior knowledge. The framework assumes learners can identify inaccuracies, verify claims, and reconstruct AI-generated outputs, yet these abilities often depend on existing disciplinary knowledge and metacognitive skills. Additional discussion is needed regarding novice versus expert learners, equity implications, prerequisite competencies, and the instructional scaffolds necessary to support effective implementation. The manuscript should also clarify the role of prompt literacy, as the quality of AI-generated artifacts depends heavily on the quality of the initial prompts.
Third, the manuscript would benefit from a deeper discussion of disciplinary applicability, limitations, and boundary conditions. Verification and reconstruction differ substantially across domains such as STEM, humanities, and social sciences, and implementation challenges may vary accordingly. The paper should engage more extensively with recent peer-reviewed research on human-AI collaboration, cognitive offloading, prompt literacy, and AI-supported learning. Addressing these issues would strengthen both the theoretical rigor and practical impact of the proposed framework.

In addition to those three major issues, the manuscript contains a large number of very short paragraphs (one to two sentences). The author(s) may consider combining related ideas into more fully developed paragraphs to improve flow, coherence, and scholarly presentation. Moreover, Figure 1 could be further developed to serve as a stronger conceptual representation of the framework. The current figure appears primarily linear, while the manuscript describes a more iterative process.



Reviewer #2: This paper addresses a timely and important topic, proposing a novel framework that reframes learners' role in AI-mediated environments from passive recipients to critical reconstructors of knowledge. The framework shows promise, particularly in its articulation of explainable ownership as a learning outcome. However, several conceptual concerns need to be addressed before the manuscript is ready for publication, as outlined in the comments below.

1.      the framework lacks an agentic account of the learner
The proposed framework sequences learner behaviors (skepticism, verification, iterative prompting) but does not theorize the conditions under which learners actually engage in these behaviors. The framework implicitly assumes a critically motivated learner, yet many students use GenAI tools primarily for efficiency. Without addressing learner agency, motivation, and goal orientation, the framework risks describing an idealized process that does not reflect realistic learner behavior. The authors should draw on established motivational or self-regulated learning theories, including Zimmerman's SRL model or expectancy-value theory, to theorize what activates skepticism and sustains critical engagement throughout the process. Specifically, the authors should address: What triggers a learner to question an AI-generated artifact rather than accept it? What dispositional, contextual, or task-level conditions make the full process more or less likely to unfold?

2.      the framework reads as a process model rather than a conceptual framework
The seven components (AI-generated artifact, learner skepticism, verification, iterative prompting, contextual integration, human reconstruction, and explainable ownership) are mainly presented as a linear sequence, but the relationships and mechanisms connecting these components are not theorized. A conceptual framework should specify not only what the constructs are, but how and why they relate. As currently presented, the paper is more accurately described as an instructional workflow or process model, which is a valid but more modest contribution. The authors should either reposition the paper honestly as a process model or substantially revise to specify the mechanisms connecting constructs. For example: Is verification a prerequisite for iterative prompting, or can they occur simultaneously? Can learners skip or regress across stages? What are the potential relationships between these constructs?

3.      the construct of "explainable ownership" is underdeveloped
Explainable ownership appears to be the most novel and potentially most significant contribution of this framework, yet it receives the least theoretical development. As written, it is unclear whether ownership refers to authorship (I produced this), epistemic ownership (I understand this), or identity-level ownership (this reflects my thinking) — these are meaningfully distinct constructs with different theoretical implications and different measurement approaches. For example, what kind of ownership does the framework refer to? Are all these three types of ownership needed for all tasks, why or why not?

4.      The tables and reference list do not consistently follow APA 7th edition guidelines.
For instance, tables should display only horizontal rules — vertical lines and internal borders should be removed. In the reference list, journal titles and volume numbers should be italicized. The authors should carefully review both sections against APA 7th edition standards prior to resubmission.
