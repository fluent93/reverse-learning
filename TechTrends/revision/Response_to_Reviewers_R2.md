# Response to Reviewers

Manuscript: "From AI-Generated Output to Learner Ownership: A Reverse Learning Framework for Generative AI-Mediated Education"

We thank the editor and both reviewers for their constructive guidance. We revised the conceptual core, relationships among components, account of learner agency, construct of explainable ownership, implementation boundaries, figure, tables, references, and presentation. Addressing the reviewers' requests while bringing the manuscript within the journal's length guidance (approximately 5,000–6,000 words) required extensive condensation and rewriting. To preserve readability and make the substantive revision path easy to review, yellow highlighting is focused on the principal reviewer-responsive additions and conceptual revisions. Locations below use stable section, proposition, table, and figure identifiers so that they remain accurate if Word pagination reflows.

[ITEM]
SOURCE: Reviewer #1
COMMENT: (1.1) Strengthen the framework's theoretical contribution and novelty. Explain the new mechanism, why existing frameworks are insufficient, how the foundations work together, and what educational phenomena Reverse Learning uniquely captures.
RESPONSE: We added Section 3.5, "An Integrated Account: The Fluency–Validity Gap." It assigns distinct functions to AI literacy, metacognition/ICAP, SRL and expectancy-value theory, knowledge building, and productive failure. It defines the fluency–validity gap as a potential discrepancy between apparent adequacy and evidential validity, contextual fit, or the learner's capacity to justify the artifact. The revision explains that no single adjacent framework was designed to center the conjunction of a complete-appearing AI-generated artifact and absent learner-held warrant; it does not claim that those theories are incapable of addressing individual parts. Section 3.4 further clarifies the pedagogical reversal: generative AI can place artifact production before understanding. The mechanism accounts for artifact-first learning, pseudo-competence, and ownership without sole origination. Section 5.1 then formalizes the mechanism as five relational propositions.

SOURCE: Reviewer #1
COMMENT: (1.2) Address readiness and prior knowledge, novice-expert differences, equity, prerequisite competencies, instructional scaffolds, and prompt literacy.
RESPONSE: New Section 6, "Learner Readiness, Prerequisites, and Scaffolding," specifies baseline domain knowledge, source evaluation, metacognitive monitoring, and prompt literacy. It explains that novices verify against curated materials and worked examples rather than unaided expertise; uses the expertise-reversal effect to justify fading supports; and identifies claim-flagging templates, source sets, verification logs, prompt stems, modeling, and revision-rationale scaffolds. Prompt literacy is addressed in Sections 5.2 and 6 as affecting both initial generation and the prompting-verification loop, without treating prompt quality as warrant.

[ITEM]
SOURCE: Reviewer #2
COMMENT: (2.2) The framework reads as a linear process model. Specify how and why constructs relate, whether verification and prompting can co-occur, and whether learners can skip or regress across components.
RESPONSE: Section 5.1 now presents five propositions: P1, activation; P2, the reciprocal but externally disciplined verification-prompting loop; P3, conversion and expected regression; P4, explainable ownership as an assessed state that should predict unaided explanation and transfer; and P5, expertise- and stakes-moderated compression. P2 states that verification and prompting can occur in either order or overlap. P3 states that integration and reconstruction can expose assumptions and return the learner to inquiry. P5 distinguishes combining or internalizing functions from omitting independent verification or substantive reconstruction. Figure 1 visualizes all five relationships, and Table 1 defines the observable functions without imposing invariant timing.

[ITEM]
SOURCE: Reviewer #2
COMMENT: (2.3) Develop explainable ownership by distinguishing authorship, epistemic ownership, and identity-level ownership and explaining whether every task requires all three.
RESPONSE: New Section 5.3 and Table 2 define accountable authorship, epistemic ownership, and identity-level ownership with distinct theoretical grounding and evidence. Epistemic ownership is the required educational core across task types; accountable authorship is required for submitted work but is defined as responsible direction, disclosure, curation, and reconstruction rather than unaided origination; identity-level ownership is task-dependent and especially relevant to reflective, argumentative, and creative work. Table 2 maps the layers to disclosures and decision trails, oral defense/transfer/revision, and reflective evidence, respectively. Proposition 4 turns epistemic ownership into an empirically testable outcome rather than a sequence-completion label.
